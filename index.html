01 · Subscription & Pricing Analytics
Streaming Economics · Price Panel
U.S. Streaming Subscription Pricing Analysis, 2018–2026
67 sourced price points · 8 services · 17 tiers · nominal and real

Subscriber growth has flattened, so price and packaging are the levers left. I built a sourced price panel — every U.S. list-price change I could date and cite, 2018 to 2026 — to test two things: how much of the increase survives inflation, and whether the ad tier and the bundle are actually absorbing it.

Flagship prices compounded at 11.2% a year over the common 2022–26 window, three times CPI. About half of that survives deflation, but it is concentrated: Disney+, Apple TV and ESPN carry almost all of the real growth, while HBO Max is negative in real terms. Neither release valve works as advertised — the ad tier has risen faster than ad-free since 2022 and the discount has sat flat near 36%, while bundles discount 35 to 63%, so list price is drifting from realized price every year.

03 · Applied Econometrics · Cross-Country
Media Economics · Regression Study
Determinants of Box Office Revenue — US vs South Korea
Parallel OLS models · 646 films · 2020–2025

I built parallel regression models for two very different film markets to answer one question: what actually drives theatrical revenue — and does the answer travel across borders?

The finding is a clean contrast. In the US, budget is the engine — every extra $1M lifts revenue ~$771K, and action films convert it best. In Korea, screens are the story — distribution reach (t = 60.2) dwarfs everything else. Same global shock, two different mechanics, because market structure — producer-driven vs. exhibitor-driven — decides what matters.

04 · Time-Series Macroeconometrics
Macro Analysis · VAR / VECM
Macroeconomic Determinants of Box Office Revenue
Dynamic regression · VAR · Johansen cointegration · VECM · 2000–2025

Does the macro economy move the box office? I tested it with a full time-series stack — dynamic regression, a three-variable VAR, and a VECM — on 300+ monthly observations of box office, consumer spending (PCE), and the fed funds rate.

The result is subtle and it's the interesting part: box office is macro-sensitive in the short run but macro-independent in its correction. A consumption shock lifts revenue immediately, then fades. But when the system drifts from equilibrium, it's the broad economy that adjusts, not the film industry — ~87% of box-office variance is its own idiosyncratic dynamics.
